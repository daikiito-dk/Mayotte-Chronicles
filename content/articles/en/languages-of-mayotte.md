Ask which language people speak in Mayotte, and the honest answer is: several. French is the official language and the language of school. Most people born on the island also speak Shimaore, a Bantu language, and many speak Kibushi, a language of the Malagasy family. Arabic is heard in mosques and Quranic schools, and the languages of the neighbouring Comorian islands are spoken in many homes. This article offers a first map of that landscape.

## French, the official language

A 2018 fact sheet from the French Ministry of Education describes French as the language of the administration, of economic life and of school. Official language policy in schools has long been almost entirely French-only, even though, as the linguist Fanny Dureysseix points out, French is not the first language of most pupils.

It is also less widespread than many visitors expect. In a 2019 survey of cultural practices in Mayotte, carried out by the French Ministry of Culture and published with INSEE in 2022, 55% of residents aged 15 or over said they had a good command of French. The figure was 75% among people born in Mayotte, 89% among those born elsewhere in France, and 36% among those born abroad.

Age makes a large difference. Among all residents, 82% of 15 to 24-year-olds said they spoke French, compared with 29% of those aged 60 or over. INSEE notes that this is partly explained by the fact that schooling only became compulsory in Mayotte in 1988.

## Shimaore, "the speech of Mayotte"

The name Shimaore (often written shimaoré in French) means, literally, "the speech (shi) of Mayotte (Maore)". Like the other languages of the Comoros archipelago, it belongs to the large Bantu family, and more precisely to the Sabaki group, which also includes Swahili.

Shimaore shares many features with its Bantu relatives. Words are built by adding prefixes, infixes and suffixes to a root, so that a single verb form can express a whole sentence. Nouns fall into noun classes, each marked by a prefix that affects the words agreeing with it; Shimaore has 13 of them. Some words are recognisable across much of the Bantu world: *mwana*, "child", is one example.

Shimaore is the most widely spoken language on the island. In the 2019 survey, 75% of residents aged 15 or over said they spoke it, as did 82% of people born in Mayotte and 75% of residents born abroad. Researchers describe it as socially dominant, ahead of French. It is also changing through contact with the languages of the neighbouring islands, Shindzuani (the language of Anjouan) and Shingazidja, to the point that some speakers refer to mixed forms such as "shimaore-shindzuani".

## Kibushi, a Malagasy language in the Comoros

Kibushi (called shibushi in Shimaore) means, roughly, "speaking the way of Madagascar". It belongs to the Austronesian family, which stretches across thousands of kilometres from the Pacific to the Indian Ocean. Words such as *vatu*, "stone", or *mati*, "dead", have close relatives in Tagalog, Tahitian and Māori.

There are in fact two Kibushi varieties in Mayotte: Kibushi kisakalava and Kibushi kiantalautsi. Together they are spoken in 19 villages, most of them in the western half of the island. Kiantalautsi is the smaller of the two and is present in only three villages. Both are mutually intelligible with the languages of Madagascar, but they also have their own vocabulary, including words borrowed from French. The school is *likoli*, bread is *dipe*, and the ferry between the two main islands is *bariji*, from the French *la barge*.

Their history is long. Austronesian peoples, mainly from Borneo, began arriving in the Indian Ocean from the 7th century. Most of the villages where Kibushi is spoken today, however, were founded during waves of migration from Madagascar between the late 18th and the mid-19th century.

In the 2019 survey, 18% of residents aged 15 or over said they spoke Kibushi, and 33% of people born in Mayotte. Almost all native Kibushi speakers also speak Shimaore, while only about a quarter of native Shimaore speakers also speak Kibushi. The figures by age suggest the language is passed on less than before: 24% of island-born people aged 15 to 24 said they spoke Kibushi, compared with 42% of those aged 60 or over.

## Arabic and the Quranic school

Islam spread through the Comoros archipelago from the 9th century, together with a network of village Quranic schools that still exists today. Many children in Mayotte attend both the public school and a Quranic school, which researchers describe as a unique situation in France.

In these schools, known as the *shioni* or the *madrasa*, Arabic is the language of the text: it is taught for writing, reading and recitation. According to the same 2018 Ministry of Education fact sheet, about 30% of the population can read, recite or write Arabic without speaking it. The conversation around the text, however, can take place in a local language. In a *shioni* observed in the southern village of Kani Keli in 2020, for example, Arabic was the language being taught while Kibushi kisakalava was the language in which the teacher (*fundi*) and pupils actually talked.

## A crossroads of languages

Beyond these four, many other languages are part of daily life. INSEE notes that, according to the 2017 census, 91% of residents born abroad were born in the Comoros, and Shindzuani in particular has a significant place in many homes. Malagasy, Réunion Creole, and the foreign languages taught at school, English and Spanish, complete the picture.

In practice, people move between languages all the time. Researchers have observed nursery-school assistants speaking the language of the village or neighbourhood with children, and teachers relying on the closeness between Shimaore and Shindzuani to communicate with pupils from Anjouan.

## School and recognition

For a long time, the French legal provisions on teaching regional languages did not apply in Mayotte. That changed in 2021. A law of May 21, 2021 repealed the article of the Education Code that had excluded Mayotte, and a circular of December 14, 2021 added Kibushi and Shimaore to the list of regional languages that can be taught in French schools. A writing system for both languages had already been approved by the Departmental Council on March 3, 2020, and the regional office of the Ministry of Culture supports publishing and cultural projects that use it.

Recognition on paper has not yet turned into regular teaching. As of 2024, researchers note that Shimaore and Kibushi were still not formally taught in schools or in teacher training, apart from a few experimental nursery classes. An evaluation found that in the 2021 to 2022 school year, only 9 of the territory's 221 schools had genuinely tested bilingual teaching. The languages are, however, taught to adults in some training centres.

## A note for travellers

French will get you through most practical situations, from the airport to the pharmacy. But the survey figures above are a reminder that many residents, especially older people, are more at ease in Shimaore or Kibushi.

You may be hoping for a short list of greetings. We have chosen not to print one yet. Spellings and usage vary, and we would rather have them checked by speakers before publishing them. They will come with our future Shimaore and Kibushi editions. Until then, the best phrase book is a patient conversation: ask, listen, and let people teach you.
