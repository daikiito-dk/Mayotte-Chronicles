Mayotte is often described in a single line: France's 101st department, in the Indian Ocean. That line is accurate, but it leaves out almost everything interesting. The island's past runs through Swahili trade, a sultanate, Malagasy migrations, a colonial cession, and a series of votes in which its inhabitants made a choice different from that of the rest of the Comoros archipelago. This article follows that story, up to the natural and political events of recent years.

## A crossroads of the Indian Ocean

Mayotte is one of the four islands of the Comoros archipelago, at the north-eastern entrance to the Mozambique Channel, between the African coast and Madagascar. The Departmental Council describes it as a crossroads of civilisations that has formed a distinct political entity since the 9th century.

Its early history is still poorly documented, but the broad outlines are known. From the 7th century, Austronesian peoples, mainly from Borneo, began arriving in the Indian Ocean, bringing new crops, boats and languages. From the 9th century, Islam spread through the archipelago, together with a network of village Quranic schools that still exists. The culture that emerged, as archaeologists from France's national institute for preventive archaeology (Inrap) put it, carries Swahili, Austronesian and Bantu influences.

## The sultanate and the mosque of Tsingoni

One of the best places to read this history is the old mosque of Tsingoni, on the island's west coast. Excavations by Inrap have found traces of occupation from the 13th century, together with a first stone mosque of the Swahili type, with a single prayer hall and no minaret tower.

From the 16th century, Tsingoni grew considerably when it became the capital of the sultanate of Mayotte, marking the beginning of its separation from the neighbouring island of Anjouan. The mosque was enlarged, and a new carved *mihrab*, the niche indicating the direction of Mecca, was built in 1538. Written sources indicate a sharp decline of Tsingoni from the 18th century. Listed as a historic monument in 2015, the mosque is considered the oldest on French national territory.

Between the late 18th and the mid-19th century, waves of migration from Madagascar founded many of the villages where Kibushi, a Malagasy language, is still spoken today.

## 1841: Mayotte becomes French

The last sultan of Mayotte, Andriantsoly, was a former king of Boeny, on the west coast of Madagascar. In April 1841, he ceded Mayotte to France, then ruled by King Louis-Philippe; the French side was represented by Commandant Passot. Sources differ slightly on the exact day: the Departmental Council gives April 26, while other references cite a treaty dated April 25. The Council notes that the cession was made without the use of force, and even before Nice and Savoy became French.

Mayotte was first administered by military governors, then became a separate colony. Its history in the following decades also included hardship: in 1856, according to the oral history of the village of Poroani, one of its founders, Bacar Koussou, led a revolt of indentured workers.

## Part of the Comoros

The other three islands of the archipelago came under French control later, through treaties signed in 1886. On March 30, 1896, France established a protectorate over the whole archipelago, Mayotte included. In 1908, Mayotte was attached to the government-general of Madagascar, and a law of July 25, 1912 confirmed the annexation of the Comoros under Madagascar's administration.

After the Second World War, in 1946, the Comoros became a French overseas territory with Dzaoudzi, on Petite-Terre, as its capital. In 1958, the decision was taken to move the capital to Moroni, on Grande Comore. According to the education researcher Fanny Dureysseix, France had invested very little in schooling in Mayotte during the colonial period, and the situation worsened after this move.

## 1974 to 1976: a different choice

In December 1972, the party in favour of independence won the elections in the Comoros, and in June 1973 France agreed to consult the population. The vote took place on December 22, 1974. Across the archipelago, independence won by a wide margin: 154,184 votes in favour and 8,853 against. Mayotte, however, voted the other way, with 8,783 votes against independence and 5,110 in favour.

A French law of July 3, 1975 then required that a new constitution be approved island by island. Three days later, on July 6, 1975, the Chamber of Deputies of the Comoros proclaimed independence, and the new state was admitted to the United Nations on November 12, 1975. France recognised the independence of the three islands where the "yes" had won and organised a new consultation in Mayotte.

On February 8, 1976, 17,845 voters chose to remain within the French Republic, against 104 who chose to join the Comorian state. A second consultation on April 11, 1976 dealt with the island's status: voters rejected the status of overseas territory and called for that of a department. A law of December 24, 1976 gave Mayotte a special status as a "territorial collectivity", which was meant to be temporary.

The Comoros' constitutions continue to state that Mayotte, or Maore, is part of the Comoros, and the disagreement between the two states remains unresolved.

## The long road to becoming a department

The question of status took decades to settle. On January 27, 2000, an agreement on the future of Mayotte was signed in Paris, and on July 2, 2000, voters approved it with 72.93% of the votes cast. Mayotte became a "departmental collectivity" in 2001, and it was named in the French Constitution in 2003.

On March 29, 2009, 95.2% of voters approved the proposal to make Mayotte a department. On March 31, 2011, Mayotte officially became France's 101st department and its fifth overseas department. The European Union followed: by a decision of the European Council of July 11, 2012, Mayotte stopped being an "overseas country and territory" and became an outermost region of the EU on January 1, 2014, giving it access to European funds.

## A volcano is born

In May 2018, the island began to shake. Hundreds of earthquakes were recorded, and on May 15, 2018, a magnitude 5.8 earthquake struck. For people in Mayotte, an island not usually prone to such activity, it was an unprecedented and worrying experience.

A year later, in May 2019, an oceanographic campaign found the cause: a new submarine volcano, about 50 kilometres east of Mayotte, rising roughly 800 metres from a seabed some 3,500 metres deep. It was named Fani Maoré. Magma movements had shifted the island about 24 centimetres eastward and caused it to subside by about 19 centimetres. A dedicated monitoring network, REVOSIMA, was set up on June 4, 2019, and still follows the area's seismic and volcanic activity today.

## Cyclone Chido and after

On December 14, 2024, Cyclone Chido crossed Mayotte as an intense tropical cyclone. Météo-France recorded gusts of 226 km/h at Pamandzi, and considers it the most violent impact on the island probably since a cyclone in February 1934. A month later, on January 18 and 19, 2025, Tropical Storm Dikeledi struck the weakened territory again. According to the French Ministry of the Interior, 90% of the island's infrastructure was damaged.

The official toll established by the prefecture two months after the cyclone was 40 deaths and 41 people missing, a toll that was not considered final. Many homes, especially in informal neighbourhoods, were destroyed, and the human cost of the disaster remains a sensitive subject on the island.

Two laws followed: an emergency law for Mayotte on February 24, 2025, and a programming law "for the refoundation of Mayotte" on August 11, 2025, with an investment plan running to 2031. An organic law adopted the same day created the "Département-Région de Mayotte", with institutional changes taking effect from the next general renewal of departmental councils. In 2026, reconstruction was still under way, with projects including a new airport at Bouyouni and a desalination plant at Ironi Bé.

History in Mayotte is not a closed chapter. The choices made in the 1970s, the slow construction of a department, and the reconstruction after Chido are all still shaping daily life on the island. We will return to many of these episodes in more detail in future articles.
